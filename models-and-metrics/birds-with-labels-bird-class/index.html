    Bird Class and Names¶

    The Birds-With-Labels model has a class that is no species: bird. It finds the calls, and the species classes name them. This page explains why it is there, how it is trained, how a box gets its name and how far the names can be trusted.

    Why a Bird Class¶

    A detector with one class per species finds a call only if it recognises the species. Two things work against it:

    • Every species learns from its own boxes alone. The training split has 559,368 call boxes. The median species has 134 of them, and 160 species have fewer than 50.
    • Unsure means several low scores. Where several species are possible, each of their classes gives the call a low score. A threshold low enough to find the call then draws one box per species.

    Just-Bird has one class, so every box teaches it what a call looks like. The bird class brings the same signal into the species model:

    A lower threshold helps the species classes only in part. At 0.05 the previous model covers as many of Just-Bird's boxes in 14 soundscapes from other places (77%) as the bird class does. But then 27% of its boxes lie where Just-Bird has none, against 7% for the bird class.

    How It Learns¶

    Step 3 of the training recipe adds bird as the last class (id 633). Three things change in the training loss:

    flowchart LR
  box["A call label,<br/>Ovenbird"]
  assign["Positions in the image<br/>that fit the box best,<br/>ranked by the bird score"]
  bird["Target for bird"]
  species["Target for Ovenbird"]
  shape["Target for the box"]
  teacher["Species teacher"]
  all["All species scores"]
  box --> assign
  assign --> bird
  assign --> species
  assign --> shape
  teacher -. distillation .-> all
    1. Every box trains two classes, bird and its species. Boxes of unknown species (????) train bird as well.
    2. The bird score decides which positions learn a box. YOLO assigns each label to the positions in the image that already predict it best, by class score and overlap (task-aligned assignment [4]). Here the class score is always that of bird. So the boxes follow the calls, not the species.
    3. Distillation goes on. The species teacher, which is the previous model, still teaches the species scores. It has no bird class, so bird learns from the labels alone.

    The new class needs a start. With random weights it would need most of the ten epochs to react at all. It starts as the mean of the trained species classes instead, which already responds to what the calls of many species share.

    From Score to Name¶

    For each 3 s window the model gives every box a bird score and 633 other scores:

    1. The bird score finds the box. Boxes above the confidence threshold (0.21) go through non-maximum suppression on that score, across all species. So one call gets one box.
    2. The boxes around it vote on the name. For each species, the highest score counts among the boxes that overlap it at IoU 0.5 or more.
    3. The top species names the box if its score is at least 0.25. Otherwise the box is named bird. The output keeps the three best species as alternatives.

    Species Names describes how names survive the merge of overlapping windows.

    Asking the neighbours in point 2 is better than reading the box's own species scores. On the song-labelled tests it adds 0.010 to 0.021 to macro F1. On the 14 soundscapes from other places, 39% of the boxes have a species score of at least 0.1, against 26%.

    How Reliable Are the Names¶

    Every box of the test sets was compared with the labels. A box lies on a call when at least half of it is inside a label, or at least half of a label is inside it. Right and wrong are counted only for boxes on a call of a known species. Boxes off any label are left out, because the labels cannot say what they are. The boxes are those before merging:

    Test set Boxes with a name Named boxes off any label Named boxes on a labelled call: right bird boxes on a labelled call: first guess right
    Five datasets 45% 11% 94% 78%
    Bird-Sounds-Global 44% 43% 92% 57%
    Powdermill (new place) 19% 7% 74% 35%
    Colombia and Costa Rica (new place) 9% 43% 68% 13%
    • On familiar ground a name on a labelled call can be trusted. More than nine in ten are right.
    • Many boxes lie off any label. On Bird-Sounds-Global and in Colombia and Costa Rica it is 43% of the named boxes. Some may be birds that nobody labelled, others may be wrong. The labels cannot tell which.
    • In a new place a name is a hint. About seven in ten on a labelled call are right, and the model names far fewer boxes. It says bird instead, which is what the class is for.
    • The guess on a bird box is often right on familiar ground, less so elsewhere. The Demo shows it as maybe.
    • Colombia and Costa Rica is the hardest case. Only 29 of its 90 species are among the model's classes. Every other bird there can only be named wrongly or bird.

    The label threshold sets the balance. A lower one names more boxes and gets more of them wrong:

    The dashed line is the default, 0.25. It scored best on the validation splits for presence per recording (macro F1 0.591 at 0.15, 0.627 at 0.25, 0.617 at 0.35) and for bout-aware matching. --label-conf sets another one.

    When names matter most

    In a place the model was not trained on, raise --label-conf to 0.45. On Powdermill 92% of the names on a labelled call are then right, and 7% of the boxes get one.
